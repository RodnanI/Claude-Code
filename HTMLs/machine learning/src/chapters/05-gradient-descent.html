Gradient descent

The engine inside nearly every modern model. Walk downhill on the loss, one small step at a time.

Picture the loss as a landscape. Every possible setting of the parameters is a location, and the height at that location is the loss. Training means finding a low point. The catch: you are standing in thick fog. You cannot see the valley, only feel the slope under your feet. So you do the obvious thing: take a step in the steepest downhill direction, feel again, repeat.

θ ← θ − η ∇L(θ)Parameters move against the gradient. η (the learning rate) sets the step size.

That single line trains linear regression, logistic regression and every neural network from a tiny classifier to the largest language models. What changes is how the gradient is computed (backpropagation, chapter 15) and a few refinements to the step.

The learning rate is the most important knob

Take steps too small and training crawls. Take them too large and you overshoot the valley floor, bounce up the other wall, and can fly off entirely. Try it on the simplest possible loss, L(w) = w².

When a training run's loss explodes to NaN, the first suspect is a learning rate that is too high. When loss decreases painfully slowly, try raising it by 3x to 10x. Sweeping learning rates on a log scale (0.1, 0.03, 0.01, 0.003…) is standard practice.

Two dimensions and better optimizers

With two parameters the landscape is a surface, drawn below as a contour map: each line joins points of equal loss, and darker shading means higher loss. Three optimizers race from the same start:

  • Gradient descent: the plain rule above.
  • Momentum: keeps a running velocity, like a heavy ball. Consistent directions build speed; zig-zags cancel out.
  • Adam: momentum plus a per-parameter step size that adapts to how large that parameter's gradients have been. The default optimizer for deep learning.

Batches, epochs, and useful noise

Computing the exact gradient needs the whole dataset, which may be millions of examples. Instead we estimate it from a small random mini-batch:

VariantGradient fromCharacter
Batch GDAll examplesAccurate, smooth, slow per step, needs the data in memory.
Stochastic GD (SGD)One exampleVery noisy, very cheap per step.
Mini-batch GD32 to a few thousand examplesThe practical default. Fits GPU parallelism well.

One pass through the whole training set is an epoch. The noise from mini-batches is not just tolerated, it helps: it can knock the optimizer out of sharp, narrow minima into wider ones, and wide minima tend to generalize better.

Learning rate schedules

Big steps help early, small steps help at the end to settle into the minimum. So the rate usually changes over training: a short warmup that ramps up from near zero (stabilizes early training of large networks), followed by decay, commonly a cosine curve down toward zero.

What about local minima?

In two dimensions local minima look scary. In the millions of dimensions of real networks, true bad local minima turn out to be rare: to trap you, a point must curve upward in every one of a million directions at once. More common are saddle points and flat plateaus, which momentum and noise help escape. For convex problems such as linear and logistic regression there is only one minimum, so gradient descent finds the global optimum.

In code

import numpy as np

X = np.random.rand(200, 1) * 10
y = 3.0 * X[:, 0] + 2.0 + np.random.randn(200)

w, b, lr = 0.0, 0.0, 0.01
for epoch in range(2000):
    y_hat = w * X[:, 0] + b
    err = y_hat - y
    grad_w = 2 * np.mean(err * X[:, 0])   # dMSE/dw
    grad_b = 2 * np.mean(err)             # dMSE/db
    w -= lr * grad_w
    b -= lr * grad_b
print(w, b)   # close to 3 and 2
import torch
model = torch.nn.Linear(10, 1)
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt, T_max=100)

Your training loss jumps around wildly and then becomes NaN. What should you try first?

Exploding loss is the classic symptom of steps that overshoot. Lower the learning rate (and check your data for extreme values).

What is an epoch?

With 10,000 examples and batch size 100, one epoch is 100 gradient steps.

Why does plain gradient descent zig-zag in a long, narrow valley?

In a narrow valley the steep walls dominate the gradient. Steps bounce between the walls while progress along the gentle floor is slow. Momentum and Adam, or simply scaling features, fix this.
