The math you actually need

Four ideas carry almost all of machine learning: vectors, matrices, derivatives and probability. You need intuition for each, not a semester of proofs.

Libraries do the arithmetic for you. What they cannot do is tell you why training diverged, why two features should be scaled, or why a 99% accurate test can still be useless. That judgment comes from understanding what the math means. This chapter builds exactly that, with a figure for each idea.

Vectors: data as arrows

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. A house with 120 m², 3 bedrooms and 15 years of age is the vector x = [120, 3, 15]. In ML, every example becomes a vector, and a dataset becomes a cloud of points in a space with one axis per feature. Two features you can draw; a thousand features you cannot, but the geometry works exactly the same.

The most important operation on vectors is the dot product: multiply matching entries and add them up.

a · b = a1b1 + a2b2 + … + anbn = |a| |b| cos θ

The first form is how you compute it. The second tells you what it means: the dot product is large when two vectors point in the same direction, zero when they are perpendicular, and negative when they point apart.

A linear model's prediction is a dot product between a weight vector and a feature vector: ŷ = w · x + b. A neuron in a neural network computes the same thing. Search engines and chatbots compare meanings by taking the cosine between embedding vectors. You will see dot products in nearly every chapter.

Matrices: many dot products at once

A matrix is a grid of numbers. Your whole dataset is a matrix X with one row per example and one column per feature. Multiplying a matrix by a vector computes one dot product per row, so X w gives predictions for every example in one shot. That is why GPUs, which are built for matrix multiplication, made deep learning practical.

There is a second, geometric way to see a matrix: it is a transformation that moves every point in space. A 2×2 matrix tells you where the two basis arrows land, and everything else follows along.

Derivatives: which way is downhill?

Training a model means adjusting parameters to make the loss smaller. To do that you need to know, for each parameter: if I nudge it a little, does the loss go up or down, and how quickly? That rate is the derivative. Geometrically it is the slope of the tangent line.

f′(x) = limh→0 f(x + h) − f(x)h

Models have many parameters, so we take a derivative with respect to each one while holding the others fixed. These are partial derivatives, and stacking them gives the gradient:

∇L = [ ∂L/∂w1, ∂L/∂w2, …, ∂L/∂wn ]The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase. Step the opposite way to decrease the loss.

One more rule and you have everything deep learning needs. The chain rule says that when functions are nested, their rates of change multiply: if y = f(g(x)) then dy/dx = f′(g(x)) · g′(x). A neural network is a long chain of nested functions, and backpropagation is simply the chain rule applied efficiently from the output backward.

Probability: reasoning under uncertainty

Data is noisy and predictions are uncertain, so ML speaks probability. The essentials:

  • Distribution: how likely each value is. The normal (Gaussian) bell curve shows up everywhere because sums of many small effects tend toward it.
  • Mean μ: the center. Variance σ²: the average squared distance from the mean. Standard deviation σ: its square root, in the original units.
  • Conditional probability P(A | B): the probability of A given that B happened. Classifiers output exactly this: P(spam | the words in this email).

Bayes' rule lets you flip a conditional around:

P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A)P(B)

It sounds abstract until you try it. A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches 90% of sick people and falsely flags 5% of healthy ones. You test positive. What is the chance you are sick? Most people, including many doctors, guess around 90%. Count it out below.

In code

import numpy as np

a = np.array([3.0, 1.0])
b = np.array([1.0, 2.5])
print(a @ b)                        # dot product: 5.5
cos = a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

X = np.random.randn(100, 3)         # 100 examples, 3 features
w = np.array([0.5, -1.0, 2.0])
y_hat = X @ w                       # 100 predictions in one line

M = np.array([[1.0, 0.5], [0.0, 1.0]])
print(np.linalg.det(M))             # 1.0: shear keeps area

Research roles expect fluent linear algebra, multivariable calculus and probability. Two embedding vectors have a cosine similarity close to 0. What does that suggest?

A cosine near 0 means the vectors are close to perpendicular: they share little direction, which in embedding space means little related meaning.

A positive slope means the loss rises as the weight rises, so move the weight the other way. That is the minus sign in gradient descent.

A classifier flags 2% of legitimate transactions as fraud and catches 95% of real fraud. Fraud is 1 in 1,000 transactions. Of the flagged transactions, roughly how many are real fraud?

Per 100,000 transactions: 100 frauds, 95 caught. 99,900 legitimate, about 1,998 falsely flagged. 95 / (95 + 1,998) is roughly 4.5%. Base rates dominate.
