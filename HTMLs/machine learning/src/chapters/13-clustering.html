Clustering

No labels, no right answers. Just data, and the question: which of these things belong together?

Most of the world's data has no labels. Clustering finds groups of similar examples on its own: customer segments for marketing, families of similar documents, groups of genes that behave alike, typical patterns of sensor readings. It is also a powerful exploration tool: cluster first, then look at what each cluster has in common.

k-means

The most widely used clustering algorithm fits in four lines:

  1. Pick k, the number of clusters, and drop k centers somewhere.
  2. Assign every point to its nearest center.
  3. Update every center to the average position of its assigned points.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until nothing changes.

Each step can only reduce the total squared distance from points to their centers, called the inertia, so the algorithm always converges. Not necessarily to the best answer, though.

Choosing k

  • Elbow method: inertia always falls as k grows (k = number of points gives zero), so look for the bend where extra clusters stop paying off.
  • Silhouette score: for each point, compares the distance to its own cluster with the distance to the nearest other cluster. Ranges from −1 to 1; higher is better.
  • The business question: often the decisive factor. A marketing team that can run four campaigns needs four segments.

k-means quietly assumes clusters are round blobs of similar size and density, because it only uses distance to a center. Try "Uneven" and "Moons" in the figure: it slices the big blob in two and cuts the moons straight across. It is also sensitive to feature scale and to outliers, which drag centers around.

DBSCAN: clusters as dense regions

DBSCAN defines clusters by density instead of centers. A point is a core point if at least minPts points (itself included) lie within distance eps. Core points that are within reach of each other chain together into one cluster. Points near a cluster but not dense themselves are border points; everything else is noise.

Hierarchical clustering

Agglomerative clustering starts with every point as its own cluster and repeatedly merges the two closest clusters. The full history of merges forms a tree called a dendrogram; cutting it at different heights gives different numbers of clusters. It is great for small datasets where the nested structure itself is interesting (species, document topics), but costs at least O(n²) memory.

Soft clustering and anomalies

Gaussian mixture models generalize k-means: each cluster is a stretched bell shape, and each point gets a probability of belonging to each cluster rather than a hard label. Closely related, anomaly detection flags points that fit no group well: DBSCAN's noise points, points with low probability under a mixture model, or the output of dedicated methods like isolation forests, which isolate outliers with few random splits.

With no labels there is no accuracy to compute. Clusters are "good" if they are stable (similar when you re-run on resampled data) and useful for the decision at hand. Always inspect examples from each cluster and describe them in plain words. If you cannot, the clusters probably are not meaningful.

In code

from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, DBSCAN
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

X_s = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X)
for k in range(2, 9):
    km = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit(X_s)   # k-means++ by default
    print(k, round(km.inertia_, 1), round(silhouette_score(X_s, km.labels_), 3))

db = DBSCAN(eps=0.3, min_samples=5).fit(X_s)
print(set(db.labels_))          # -1 marks noise points

What does the k-means update step do?

The two alternating steps are assign (points to nearest center) and update (centers to the mean of their points).

Your data forms two interlocking crescent shapes. Which algorithm is more suitable?

Density-based clustering follows arbitrary shapes; k-means can only produce convex, blob-like regions around centers.

Why does inertia alone not tell you the right number of clusters?

More centers always fit better, so you look for diminishing returns (the elbow) or use silhouette and domain knowledge.
