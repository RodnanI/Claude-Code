# How LLMs Are Trained at Scale

Your mini GPT and a frontier model run the same algorithm. The differences are data, scale, engineering and post-training. You probably will not pretrain a frontier model in your career, but you will constantly make decisions that depend on understanding how models were made: which model to pick, why it behaves a certain way, whether fine-tuning can fix something.

## Stage 1: pretraining data

The model learns from what it reads. Pretraining corpora are measured in trillions of tokens, mixing:

- web pages (filtered crawls such as Common Crawl derivatives)
- code (public repositories)
- books, scientific papers, encyclopedias, forums, math
- increasingly, **synthetic data** generated by other models (textbook-style explanations, problems with solutions, rewritten web text)

Raw web text is mostly junk, so the data pipeline is a huge part of the work:

1. **Text extraction** from HTML, PDFs and so on.
2. **Language identification.**
3. **Deduplication**: exact and near-duplicate removal (MinHash and similar). Duplicates waste compute and increase memorization.
4. **Quality filtering**: heuristics (length, symbol ratios, repetition) and classifiers trained to recognize "educational" or "high quality" text.
5. **Safety and privacy filtering**: remove toxic content, personal information where possible.
6. **Decontamination**: remove benchmark test questions so evaluation stays honest.
7. **Mixing**: decide the proportions (how much code, math, each language), often tuned with small proxy models.

Lesson that transfers to every ML job: at fixed compute, better data beats more data, and data work is where much of the real advantage lives.

## Stage 2: pretraining compute and engineering

Recall: training costs about `6 x parameters x tokens` FLOPs. Frontier runs use thousands to tens of thousands of GPUs for weeks or months.

The model and its optimizer state do not fit on one GPU, so the work is split:

| Parallelism | Splits | Idea |
|-------------|--------|------|
| data parallel | the batch | every GPU has a full copy, processes different examples, gradients are averaged |
| FSDP / ZeRO | parameters, gradients and optimizer state | each GPU stores a shard, gathers what it needs just in time |
| tensor parallel | individual matrix multiplies | one layer's matrices split across GPUs |
| pipeline parallel | layers | GPU 1 runs layers 1-10, GPU 2 runs 11-20, micro-batches flow through |
| expert parallel | MoE experts | different experts live on different GPUs |
| sequence / context parallel | the sequence | long sequences split across GPUs |

Other realities of big runs: bf16 or fp8 mixed precision, frequent checkpoints (hardware fails daily at this scale), monitoring for **loss spikes**, and throughput measured as **MFU** (model FLOPs utilization: the share of the hardware's peak actually used; 40-50% is considered good).

Learning rate schedules: warmup then cosine decay (your mini GPT), or **warmup-stable-decay** (constant rate for most of training, quick decay at the end), often finishing with a short "annealing" phase on the highest-quality data.

Most pretraining runs see each token about once (one epoch). Repeating data a few times is fine; many repetitions start to hurt.

## Scaling laws

Kaplan et al. (2020) showed that loss falls as a smooth **power law** in model size, data and compute, across many orders of magnitude. You can predict the loss of a big run from small runs. This predictability is why companies were willing to spend so much: the returns were measurable in advance.

**Chinchilla** (Hoffmann et al., 2022) refined the recipe: for a fixed compute budget, the best loss comes from scaling parameters and tokens together, roughly **20 training tokens per parameter**. Many earlier models were too big and undertrained.

Since then, models are often trained far beyond "Chinchilla-optimal" (hundreds or thousands of tokens per parameter), because a smaller model trained longer is cheaper to *serve* to millions of users. Training is paid once, inference forever.

**Emergent abilities**: some capabilities seem to appear suddenly at scale. Part of this is real, part is an artifact of all-or-nothing metrics (a smooth improvement looks like a jump when you only count fully correct answers). Know both sides of the debate.

## Stage 3: post-training (turning a base model into an assistant)

### Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)

Train on examples of `(conversation so far, ideal assistant response)`, formatted with the model's chat template. The loss is usually computed only on the assistant's tokens, not the user's. Thousands to millions of examples, written by people or generated and filtered. Quality matters more than quantity: the LIMA paper (2023) got a strong assistant from only 1,000 carefully curated examples. SFT mainly teaches format and style; the knowledge was already there from pretraining.

### Preference tuning: RLHF and DPO

People are better at **comparing** two answers than writing perfect ones. So collect preferences: "for this prompt, response A is better than B".

- **RLHF** (used for InstructGPT and early ChatGPT): train a **reward model** to predict which response humans prefer, then optimize the LLM with reinforcement learning (classically PPO) to get high reward, with a **KL penalty** that keeps it close to the SFT model so it does not drift into gibberish that fools the reward model.
- **DPO** (direct preference optimization, 2023): a clever reformulation that trains directly on preference pairs with a classification-style loss. No separate reward model, no RL loop. Simpler and widely used, along with many variants.
- **RLAIF / Constitutional AI** (Anthropic): use AI feedback guided by a written set of principles to generate preferences or critiques, reducing reliance on human labeling.

### Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards

For math, code, logic puzzles and other tasks where answers can be checked automatically (run the unit tests, compare the final number), the model generates attempts and gets rewarded for correct ones. Trained this way at scale, models learn to produce long **chains of thought**: planning, checking, backtracking. This created the "reasoning model" generation from late 2024 onward. GRPO (group relative policy optimization), popularized by DeepSeek, is one widely used algorithm: sample several answers per problem and reward each relative to the group's average.

### Safety training

Teaching the model to refuse genuinely harmful requests without refusing harmless ones, to resist manipulation, and to be honest about uncertainty. Involves red teaming (people and models attacking the model on purpose), targeted training data and evaluation.

### Known side effects

- **Reward hacking**: optimizing a proxy (a reward model, a test suite) finds loopholes. A code model might learn to special-case the tests instead of solving the problem.
- **Sycophancy**: optimizing for human approval can teach the model to agree with users and flatter them. A real, studied problem.
- **Over-refusal** vs **under-refusal**: a constant balancing act.

## Distillation

Train a small "student" model to imitate a large "teacher": on the teacher's generated outputs, or on its full probability distributions (logits), which carry more information than single answers. Many fast, cheap models are made this way. Note that some providers' terms of service prohibit using their outputs to train competing models.

## Why this matters for your job

- A **base model** continues text; an **instruct model** follows instructions. Do not prompt a base model like a chatbot.
- Fine-tuning (file 20) is a small version of post-training. It changes behavior and format well; it is a poor way to add lots of new facts (use retrieval for that).
- Model behavior quirks (verbosity, hedging, refusals, sycophancy) are often fingerprints of post-training choices. Prompting and fine-tuning can shift them.
- When a model is great at benchmarks and mediocre on your task, remember Goodhart, contamination and that your task was not in anyone's training mix. Build your own evaluation (file 22).
